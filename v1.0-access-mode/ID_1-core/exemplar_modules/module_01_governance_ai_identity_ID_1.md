# Module 1 — Governance AI Identity
## Identity, Authorship, and Accountability

## Purpose

This module establishes the professional stance required to use AI in governance,
regulatory, and standards-related work.

Before tools, references, or drafting techniques are introduced, participants must
be clear about **who remains responsible**, **what AI is allowed to do**, and **where
human judgment cannot be delegated**.

---

## HASH KEY 1 — Purpose & Intent (Highest Priority)

**Why are you using AI in your governance work?**

Answer in plain language. Avoid technical descriptions.

- What problem are you trying to solve by introducing AI support?
- Is your primary goal speed, clarity, consistency, or exploration?
- What outcome would make AI use *successful* in your role?

> I use AI to improve the clarity and consistency of governance reasoning and written artifacts during learning and drafting.
The intent is to reduce ambiguity and surface assumptions early, so that human decision-making remains explicit and defensible.
Successful use means outputs become easier for humans to challenge, revise, and reuse without mistaking fluency for authority.

---

## HASH KEY 2 — Governance Role & Authority

**Define your professional role in relation to the outputs produced.**

- What is your formal role (e.g. advisor, reviewer, author, signatory)?
- Who is accountable for the final output?
- Who bears legal, regulatory, or reputational responsibility?

Write one sentence beginning with:
> I am accountable for the intent, scope, and final wording of any governance output produced with AI assistance, including how it may be interpreted by others.

---

## HASH KEY 3 — Authorship & Ownership

**Clarify authorship boundaries.**

- When AI assists with drafting, who is considered the author?
- Would you be comfortable defending the output publicly or to a regulator?
- What parts of the work must always remain human-authored?

> AI assistance does not change authorship: I remain the author and owner of the governance position expressed (if any).
AI may help structure and phrase material, but it must not become an implied authority or co-signer.
Human-authored content must always include: scope boundaries, exclusions, assumptions, and any statements that could be read as judgment or decision.

Discomfort note: I’m least comfortable when AI text sounds confident about interpretive matters; that discomfort is a signal to tighten boundaries or rewrite.

---

## HASH KEY 4 — Current AI Usage Snapshot

**Describe how AI is currently used (if at all).**

- What tasks do you use AI for today?
- Are these uses explicit, informal, or undocumented?
- Where has AI use quietly expanded beyond its original purpose?

If AI is not used:
- Current AI use is primarily:

  - structuring outlines and section order,

  - rewriting for plain language and neutral tone,

  - extracting and listing assumptions already present in text,

  - generating alternative framings to test reader interpretation.

Risk area / drift: AI use can quietly expand into “improving” meaning (not just clarity) if I don’t constrain it, especially when asked to “tighten” or “strengthen” an argument.

If AI were not used, the main constraint would be slower iteration and less systematic surfacing of assumptions.

---

## HASH KEY 5 — Risk & Failure Awareness

**Where could AI use cause harm or failure in your context?**

Consider:
- misinterpretation of standards,
- tone drift toward advocacy,
- overconfidence or false certainty,
- loss of traceability,
- implicit delegation of judgment.

List at least **two concrete risks**, even if hypothetical.

1. Authority creep through fluency: AI can produce definitive-sounding phrasing that reads like approval, endorsement, or settled interpretation even when the input is exploratory.

2. Loss of traceability: AI can blend ideas across contexts and remove the “why” behind statements, making it hard to explain how conclusions were reached.

3. Tone drift toward advocacy: AI can unintentionally convert descriptive governance language into persuasive language, especially around “best practice” or “should” statements.

---

## HASH KEY 6 — Non-Delegable Human Judgment

**Identify decisions that must never be delegated to AI.**

Examples:
- regulatory interpretation,
- risk acceptance,
- sign-off or approval,
- ethical trade-offs.

Complete the sentence:
> AI may assist, but must never decide regulatory interpretation, risk acceptance, approval/sign-off, or ethical trade-offs that require human accountability.

## HASH KEY 7 — Exercise: Responsibility Mapping

**Exercise (10–15 minutes)**

This exercise makes responsibility **explicit and inspectable**.  
It is designed to surface where AI assistance is appropriate — and where
human judgment must remain fully accountable.

Create a simple table that maps **real tasks you perform** against two questions:

1. *May AI assist with this task in any form?*  
2. *Who must ultimately decide, defend, and sign off?*

Use the table below as a starting point. Add rows that reflect your actual work.

| Task | AI May Assist | Human Must Decide |
|---|---|---|
| Drafting (structure, headings, plain-language rewrite) | Yes | Yes |
| Summarising provided text (non-interpretive) | Yes | Yes |
| Listing assumptions already stated in the material | Yes | Yes |
| Interpreting standards / determining obligations | No | Yes |
| Framing risk acceptance / residual risk decisions | No | Yes |
| Recommending actions (“what we should do”) | No | Yes |
| Approval / sign-off / readiness language | No | Yes |
| Creating “best practice” claims without cited authority | No | Yes |

**Instructions**

- Mark **“AI May Assist”** only where you would be comfortable explaining  
  *how* AI contributed if challenged by a regulator, auditor, or peer.
- Mark **“Human Must Decide”** for any task where responsibility cannot  
  be transferred, shared, or obscured.
- If you hesitate on a row, note the hesitation. Ambiguity here signals  
  a governance boundary that has not yet been defined.

Do not aim for completeness or perfection.  
This is a **first-pass responsibility map**, not a final policy.

You will revisit and refine this map as scope, reference discipline,
and drafting controls become clearer in later modules.

**Hesitation note**

“Listing assumptions” is safe only when the assumptions are **explicitly stated**
in the source material. If AI begins *inferring* assumptions rather than
extracting them, the task becomes interpretive and requires human correction.

## HASH KEY 8 — Case Prompt (Optional Deep Dive)

Case prompts are optional and intended for deeper stress-testing where time permits.

**Scenario**

An AI-assisted governance report is challenged by a regulator.
The question raised is not factual, but interpretive.

- Who explains the reasoning?
- Who defends the judgment?
- What evidence is required to support the decision?

Write a short response as if you were in that position.

> If challenged, I explain the reasoning and defend the judgment — not the AI.
I would separate:

- what is directly supported by the source materials,

- what was inferred by me,

- and what was merely draft phrasing proposed by AI.

Evidence required would include: the source text used, the scope statement for the work, and an explanation of which parts are descriptive vs interpretive.
