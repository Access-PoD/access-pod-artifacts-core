## Purpose

This module introduces **Access PoD** as a *governance lens*, not as a platform
or implementation requirement.

The goal is to help participants shift from **document-centric governance**
toward **execution-aware governance thinking**, where trust, risk, and assurance
attach to *what systems do*, not only to what they declare.

This module builds on prior work:
- defined intent and scope,
- bounded references,
- disciplined drafting,
and applies them to **runtime-oriented governance reasoning**.

---

## HASH KEY 1 — Why Execution Matters in Governance

**Why is execution relevant to governance at all?**

Consider:
- What happens after a document is published?
- How do systems behave once deployed?
- Where do risks emerge that documentation cannot capture?

> Execution matters because many governance risks and failures only become visible once systems are actually used, not when documents are written.

---

## HASH KEY 2 — From Static Artifacts to Operational Governance

**Contrast traditional governance with execution-aware governance.**

Traditional focus:
- policies,
- reports,
- attestations.

Execution-aware focus:
- behavior,
- enforcement,
- evidence over time.

Write two short lists:
- What static governance is good at
- What it consistently misses

> Static governance is good at: establishing intent, documenting decisions, and communicating expectations.
> It often misses: how controls behave over time, where drift occurs, and whether assumptions hold under real use.

---

## HASH KEY 3 — Execution Boundaries as Governance Anchors

**Identify where governance can meaningfully attach.**

Examples of execution boundaries:
- decision or inference points,
- data access or transformation,
- human-in-the-loop escalation,
- model update or configuration change.
- delegation boundaries (internal vs external models or tools)

> Governance can meaningfully attach at decision points, data access or transformation events, and moments where authority shifts between humans and systems.

---

## HASH KEY 4 — Runtime Evidence vs Declarative Assurance

**Clarify the difference between saying and showing.**

Declarative assurance answers:
- “What do we claim?”

Runtime evidence answers:
- “What actually happened?”

Reflect:
- Where do your current governance practices rely on declarations?
- Where would evidence change confidence?

> Declarative assurance answers what an organisation claims. Runtime evidence answers what actually happened and whether those claims hold in practice.

---

## HASH KEY 5 — Continuous vs Point-in-Time Trust

**Examine how trust is currently treated.**

- Is trust assumed once granted?
- Is it periodically reviewed?
- Is it revocable in practice?

> Trust in my governance context is currently treated as something granted at approval or review time. In a continuous model, trust would be revisited based on observed behavior rather than assumed to persist unchanged.

---

## HASH KEY 6 — Human Oversight at Runtime (Critical Control)

**Re-anchor human authority in execution contexts.**

Consider:
- When should humans intervene?
- What triggers escalation?
- What decisions must never be automated?

> At runtime, human oversight is required when decisions have material impact, when assumptions fail, or when system behavior deviates from documented intent.

---

## HASH KEY 7 — Exercise: Execution Mapping

**Exercise (20–25 minutes)**

Choose one system, process, or AI-assisted workflow you oversee.

Create a simple map:
- Where decisions occur
- Where policies apply
- Where evidence could be observed
- Where human intervention is possible

> In an AI-assisted workflow, decisions occur when outputs are generated, policies apply when data is accessed, evidence could be observed through logged outcomes, and human intervention is possible when results are escalated for review.

---

## HASH KEY 8 — Case Prompt (Evidence Gap)

Case prompts are optional and intended for deeper stress-testing where time permits.

**Scenario**

A governance review asks:
> “How do you know this control works in practice?”

- What evidence can you provide today?
- What evidence is missing?
- What would need to change to observe it?

> Today, evidence consists mainly of documented controls and process descriptions. What is missing is visibility into how often controls are triggered and how exceptions are handled. Observing this would require defined checkpoints rather than new enforcement.

## HASH KEY 9 — Reflection Checkpoint

Reflect:

- Which governance assumptions rely on trust rather than evidence?
- Where would execution-level visibility feel uncomfortable?
- What would improve confidence without increasing burden?

> I assume governance is effective once approved. Thinking in execution terms makes it clear that approval is only a starting point, not proof of continued effectiveness.
